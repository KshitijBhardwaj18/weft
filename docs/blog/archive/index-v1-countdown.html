One live plan for every drop

A D2C product is planned, made, sold and restocked, and each stage lives in a different tool. Weft keeps one live plan per drop, from the first sample to the next restock, as a FalkorDB graph, with AI teammates that chase the updates and show what every change breaks.

Kshitij BhardwajBuilt for FalkorDB Graph Hacks8 minute read
The problem

Thirteen days before the drop, the fabric is late

Kshitij is launching a premium fitness-wear brand. The first collection, the Black Drop, is 3 styles, 15 SKUs and 2,100 units, and it goes live on 1 December.

The leggings and the sports bra come from Sri Lakshmi Knits in Tiruppur. The tanks come from Kavya Garments in Bengaluru. Three creators, Ria, Dev and Tara, have scheduled posts telling their followers the drop goes live on 1 December.

On 18 November, Murugan at Sri Lakshmi sends one line to a WhatsApp group: the leggings fabric is delayed, coming on the 28th.

Nobody here is careless. The message arrives in a group with a dozen others. The tracker was last updated a week ago. The shop has never heard of a factory. The creator posts were agreed in a different chat, with a different person. To see that a fabric delay breaks three creator posts, someone has to hold all four tools in their head at once, and in a small brand that someone is the founder, late at night.

This is how small D2C brands lose launches and money. A drop slips and the creators who announced it look wrong in front of their followers. A size sells out in four days and the reorder never goes out, because sales live in one tool and factory lead times in another. A supplier was chosen for a reason that nobody wrote down, and three weeks later nobody can say why.

Every tool sees one slice.

Shopify sees orders. WhatsApp holds the supplier talk. A spreadsheet holds the production plan. The problems that hurt most cross from making to selling, so they stay invisible until a customer or a creator notices.

The idea

One live plan per drop, as a graph

Weft keeps one plan for each drop, and the plan is a graph in FalkorDB. Suppliers, purchase orders, SKUs, styles, the drop and the creator posts that promote it are connected nodes, and every fact links back to where it came from. When something changes anywhere, the graph already knows what sits downstream.

The plan is the source of truth, not the chat. It stays current four ways: the team edits it in the app, suppliers answer check-ins, chat messages become linked evidence, and a due date that passes with no news shows up as gone quiet.

Three AI teammates work on it with us. Weft is the chief of staff, Shuttle runs production and Pattern reads the sales. They don't keep their own notes. They share the graph as their memory, and every answer they give comes with the path behind it.

02

Shuttle asks before it is due

Shuttle, the production teammate, doesn't wait for news. The fabric for PO-014 is due today, so it has already sent Murugan a check-in: one question, answered in one tap. Murugan sees only his own POs and this chat.

He picks Delayed and keeps the date, 28 November. The answer is written straight onto the check-in node attached to PO-014 in the graph.

03

Your call, with the impact attached

Shuttle doesn't change the plan on its own. It walks the graph from PO-014 through its SKUs and style to the Black Drop, then back out to every creator post that promotes it, and turns the result into a call for Kshitij.

Fabric on 28 November means the leggings land around 5 December, four days after the drop, and the three posts that promise 1 December break. That sentence is one Cypher query, not someone's memory.

04

Try it in a scenario first

Kshitij could just accept the new date. Instead he opens a scenario: what if PO-014 moves to Kavya Garments? Weft makes a full private copy of the plan with GRAPH.COPY, moves the PO only inside the copy, and re-dates it from Kavya's 10-day lead time.

Then it asks both graphs the same question. Reality: 5 December, the drop is late, 3 posts broken. The scenario: 28 November, on time, every post kept, for ₹12,000 more. He applies it. The scenario graph is copied over the plan, and the decision is recorded with who, when and which scenario.

05

Ask why, and see why

A week from now someone will ask why the leggings moved, or why we picked Sri Lakshmi in the first place. ⌘J opens Ask. Weft answers from the graph and cites the nodes it used. See why lists every Cypher query it ran and the messages the answer traces back to.

Under the hood

Why a graph, and why FalkorDB

The questions that matter here are about connections, not records. “Which creator posts break if PO-014 is late?” is not a lookup on the PO. It is a walk: PO to SKUs to style to drop, then back in through every post that promotes it. With relational tables, each question like that is a set of joins written by hand. In a graph, the walk is the query, and the same walk can run inside every step a teammate takes.

One FalkorDB instance holds several graphs: weft is the live plan, weft_live is the day-9 snapshot, weft_meta holds settings and scenario records, and each scenario is its own weft_scn* graph. The app is Next.js 15, and its server components read the graph directly.

Multi-hop Cypher

Impact is one traversal

Every delay call runs this. It goes from the PO to its supplier and owner, through SKUs and style to the drop, then out to the creator posts that promise the date.

MATCH (po:PO {id:$po})-[:MADE_BY]->(sup:Supplier), (owner:Person)-[:OWNS]->(po),
      (po)-[:SUPPLIES]->(k:SKU)-[:OF_STYLE]->(s:Style)-[:IN_DROP]->(d:Drop)
OPTIONAL MATCH (cr:Creator)-[:POSTED]->(p:CreatorPost)-[:PROMOTES]->(d)
RETURN po.id AS po, sup.name AS supplier, collect(DISTINCT k.code) AS skus, s.name AS style,
       d.name AS drop, d.date AS dropDate, owner.name AS owner,
       collect(DISTINCT {handle: cr.handle, date: p.date}) AS posts
GRAPH.COPY

Scenarios are whole graphs

A scenario is a full copy of the plan. The change happens only in the copy. Apply to plan copies the scenario back over the plan, records the decision, and deletes the copy.

// graph.copy() issues GRAPH.COPY
await d.selectGraph(from).copy(to);

// inside the copy only
MATCH (po:PO {id:$po})-[r:MADE_BY]->(:Supplier), (to:Supplier {id:$to})
DELETE r
CREATE (po)-[:MADE_BY]->(to)
SET po.eta = $eta, po.unitPrice = po.unitPrice + 20, po.status = 'accepted'
Graph algorithm

PageRank finds the single point of failure

PageRank over the MADE_BY edges ranks the suppliers the drop leans on most. It is how Today knows that Sri Lakshmi Knits carries 9 of the 15 SKUs.

CALL algo.pageRank(null, 'MADE_BY') YIELD node, score
WITH node, score WHERE node:Supplier
OPTIONAL MATCH (node)<-[:MADE_BY]-(:PO)-[:SUPPLIES]->(k:SKU)
RETURN node.name AS name, score, count(k) AS skus ORDER BY score DESC
Full-text index

Search lives on the same graph

Full-text indexes over SKUs, styles, suppliers, POs and creators power Brand map search. Searching “olive” lights up every matching node.

await g.createNodeFulltextIndex("SKU", "code", "colour", "line");

CALL db.idx.fulltext.queryNodes($label, $term) YIELD node
RETURN node.id AS id
GRAPH.RO_QUERY

Teammates query the graph, they never write to it

Weft, Shuttle and Pattern are one Claude model in a tool loop of up to eight turns. Its only tool is run_cypher, which takes a query and a one-line reason. Every call runs through GRAPH.RO_QUERY, so a teammate can read anything and change nothing. Each query is recorded with its row count and shown under See why. Plan changes go through calls that a person approves.

{ name: "run_cypher",
  description: "Run a read-only openCypher query against the brand's FalkorDB graph ...",
  input_schema: { properties: { query: { type: "string" }, why: { type: "string" } } } }

// every tool call
await (await db()).selectGraph(graph).roQuery(cypher, { params });   // GRAPH.RO_QUERY
One honest lesson

We first wrote See why paths with shortestPath(). In FalkorDB it follows edges in their stored direction only, and our paths run both ways: a decision points at a PO, a creator post points at the drop. So See why uses a bounded variable-length match, ordered by length:

MATCH (a {id:$a}), (b {id:$b})
MATCH p = (a)-[*1..5]-(b)
WITH p, length(p) AS len ORDER BY len LIMIT 1
RETURN [n IN nodes(p) | n.id] AS ids
